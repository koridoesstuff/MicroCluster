Privacy policy

This describes the mechanism as built. It is not legal boilerplate.

MicroCluster is a privacy-preserving health-signal system for small shared communities. It looks for statistically unusual symptom activity and discloses it only at a scope that is both statistically supported and privacy-safe. The running page on this site is an illustrative simulation of that system: the people, the outbreak, and every parameter are synthetic.

What a real deployment collects

Each report is a single submission from a fixed checklist:

  • one of four symptom categories (respiratory, gastrointestinal, general, other);
  • a specific symptom from that category, recorded for the reporter's own clarity and never used for matching;
  • a coarse onset bucket: today, 1–2 days, 3–7 days, 1+ week;
  • one location group from the community's scope hierarchy, at the finest level the reporter belongs to.

What it never collects

  • No name, account, login, device identifier, or contact information.
  • No free text. Every field is a choice from a closed vocabulary, so there is nothing to interpret and nothing to leak in prose.
  • No precise timestamp. Onset is a bucket; submission time is only used to decide whether a report falls inside the current detection window.
  • No precise location. The finest location is a declared group, not a coordinate or a room.
  • No medical concept finer than a category. The system has no code path that reasons about a specific diagnosis.

The privacy strategy is data minimisation, not encryption. The system never holds identity, free text, precise time, or precise location, so there is very little to encrypt. You cannot leak what you did not collect.

How the gates work

Detection and disclosure are separate. Detection decides whether something unusual is happening. Disclosure decides what, if anything, may be said, and at what scope. A scope is disclosed only if it passes both of two gates, which are evaluated independently and never combined into one number:

Evidence gate. The bigger the group you want to name, the more reports you need before the system will name it, and never fewer than 5.

In symbols: needed=max(5,⌈n⌉), where n is how many people are in the group.

Worked numbers load from the running server.

Privacy gate. The system never names a group with fewer than 20 people, and never names a group where more than half of the members have reported, because that announcement would be close to a list of who is ill.

In symbols, with q reports from a group of n people: n≥20 and qn≤0.5.

Worked numbers load from the running server.

The two gates pull in opposite directions by design. The system discloses the finest scope that clears both; if none does, it discloses nothing. Every candidate scope, including the rejected ones, gets a full evaluation record with the reason for its outcome. The disclosure engine is only ever handed aggregate per-scope counts and declared populations, never individual reports.

What the gates guarantee, and what they do not

In established terms, the two gates together enforce three things about every group the system names, every time, by construction:

  • A group-size floor. The named group has at least 20 people. This is a k-anonymity-style property (k of at least 20): the statement is about a group, and any member is indistinguishable from the others in it, because reports carry nothing finer than a group.
  • Minimum support. The statement rests on at least 5 reports, more for bigger groups. This is a standard small-count threshold rule.
  • A prevalence cap. At most half of the group can be among the reporters, so knowing someone is in the named group never makes them more than 50% likely to be a reporter. This limits the homogeneity weakness of k-anonymity. It is a simple cap, not a full l-diversity or t-closeness guarantee.

That is all the gates promise. They do not provide differential privacy. Differential privacy adds calibrated random noise so that any one person's data changes what is released by at most a bounded amount. These gates add no noise and use hard thresholds on exact counts: one extra report can be the difference between naming a group and saying nothing, so the change in output from one person is not bounded. They also keep no privacy budget, so many daily releases over overlapping data are not accounted for, and they give no protection against someone who already knows almost all of the reports.

They also do not guarantee protection from the operator who holds the reports, do not stop an attacker who injects reports, do not verify that a group's declared size is true, and do not make being named as a group with unusual illness harmless to its members. The full write-up is in docs/privacy_guarantee.md in the repository.

What the bands are for

The interface shows qualifying report counts as ranges (“5–9”, “10–19”), never exact integers. This is a defence against a differencing attack: if the exact count for a named group were visible on two consecutive days, an observer could subtract them and learn that exactly one identifiable person changed state overnight. The exact count stays on the server; only the band is sent to the page. This reduces that leak; it does not eliminate every inference. A named group with sustained activity in a known band still tells an observer something, and the system's own adversarial self-test reports exactly what.

Abuse

Because reports are anonymous, someone can submit reports to manufacture a signal. The system accepts this as the cost of anonymity. It rate-limits submissions per session (a per-session and a per-session-per-day cap, enforced server side), but it does not authenticate reporters, so a determined attacker using multiple sessions can still inject reports. No identity-based control is used, because identity is the thing the system exists to protect.
